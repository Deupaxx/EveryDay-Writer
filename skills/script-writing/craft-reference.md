# EW Script Writing - Craft Reference

Use this reference for serious script work, especially when the request involves feature screenplays, TV episodes, short films, YouTube scripts, video essays, documentaries, sketches, or social video.

This is a source-informed synthesis of the user's supplied screenwriting, YouTube scripting, and writing craft transcripts, plus durable screen/story craft principles. Do not quote the source transcripts. Use the ideas as diagnostics and workflow guidance.

---

## Format Selection

Before writing, identify the script type. If the user does not specify and the format changes the output, ask one concise question.

| Request | Output mode |
|---|---|
| Feature film, screenplay, movie scene | Screenplay pages |
| TV pilot, episode, procedural, sitcom | TV script pages or beat outline |
| Short film | Compressed screenplay scene |
| Sketch | Setup/escalation/payoff script |
| YouTube video, educational video, personal brand | YouTube long-form script |
| Documentary, breakdown, reaction essay | Documentary/video essay script |
| TikTok, Reel, Short | Short-form social script |
| Voiceover for edit | VO script with edit notes |
| "Script this idea" with no format | Ask: screen, YouTube, short-form, or stage/podcast? |

Screenplay rules and YouTube rules are not interchangeable. A screenplay is a blueprint for production. A YouTube script is an attention and satisfaction machine. Both need story, but they optimize different surfaces.

---

## Universal Script Standard

Every script must do four things:

1. Give the audience a reason to stay.
2. Move through visible or audible change.
3. Make the next beat feel necessary.
4. End with a changed situation, changed belief, changed question, or changed desire.

If a beat does not alter what the viewer knows, expects, feels, fears, or wants, it is probably dead air.

## Reader And Viewer Value

Do not optimize only for clarity. A script can be clear and still useless.

Before drafting, answer:

- Who is this for?
- What do they already expect from the title, premise, genre, or setup?
- What would make them feel this was worth their time?
- What problem, desire, fear, curiosity, or emotional hunger does the script serve?
- What is the specific change the script should create in them?

For screenplays, "value" often means emotional involvement: the reader cares what happens next. For YouTube, it often means satisfaction: the viewer learned something, felt something, or was entertained enough to keep watching and share.

## Screenplay Core

### The Three Non-Negotiables

A viable screen story needs:

- A sympathetic or at least emotionally legible central character.
- A visible, physical, high-stakes goal.
- A powerful adversary or opposition force committed to stopping that goal.

If one is missing, diagnose before drafting. A mood, world, or character sketch is not yet a movie.

### Build From The Ending Backward

Before writing a full feature or short, know the emotional destination:

- Where does the final confrontation happen?
- What must the protagonist choose?
- What does winning cost?
- What image tells us the story is over?

The ending can change later, but drafting without a destination usually produces a middle that wanders.

### Hero Goal Sequences

For feature-style plotting, think in units of active change:

- The protagonist pursues one short-term visible goal.
- The attempt creates conflict.
- New information, a reversal, a discovery, or a consequence ends that goal.
- The next short-term goal becomes necessary.

Use this as a diagnostic, not a cage. If the script sags, check whether too many pages pass without a concrete short-term goal or fresh news.

### Passive Hero Test

A passive protagonist is one of the fastest ways to kill a screenplay.

Ask:

- What is the protagonist actively trying to do in this sequence?
- What decision do they make under pressure?
- What happens because they chose, not merely because something happened to them?

If the protagonist only receives events, rewrite the sequence so they pursue, choose, risk, or refuse.

## Screenplay Formatting

### Slug Lines

Use slug lines to orient production:

```text
INT. LOCATION - TIME
EXT. LOCATION - TIME
INT./EXT. LOCATION - TIME
```

Time is usually `DAY`, `NIGHT`, `LATER`, `MOMENTS LATER`, or `CONTINUOUS`.

### Action Lines

Action lines are present-tense, visual, and concise.

- Introduce major characters in ALL CAPS on first appearance.
- Capitalize major sounds or important production elements when useful.
- Keep action blocks short; four lines is usually the ceiling.
- Prefer behavior over explanation.
- Use strong verbs.

### Dialogue Notation

- `V.O.`: voiceover, not physically in the scene's space.
- `O.S.`: off screen, in the scene but not visible.
- `O.C.`: off camera, present but outside the camera frame.
- Parentheticals only clarify genuinely ambiguous delivery or small business.

Avoid parentheticals that tell actors how to act when the line and context should already carry it.

### Transitions

Use transitions sparingly. Most scenes do not need `CUT TO:`. Include transitions only when the cut itself carries meaning, rhythm, comedy, or production intent.

## Character Drama

Build characters from the inside out:

1. Core values: what they protect, worship, fear losing, or cannot betray.
2. Inner contradiction: two values they cannot hold at the same time under pressure.
3. Mannerisms: repeatable behaviors that reveal and hide those values.
4. Traits: the visible surface the audience first notices.

Drama appears when pressure forces a choice:

- Person against self: one value must betray another.
- Person against person: two sympathetic value systems collide.
- Person against world: the character's desired life conflicts with nature, history, society, or metaphysical order.

Do not make characters "complex" by adding traits. Make them complex by giving them values that become incompatible when the story tightens.

## Dialogue

Dialogue is stylized pressure, not transcription.

Good dialogue often:

- Sounds speakable aloud.
- Carries subtext.
- Moves power between characters.
- Lets people dodge the actual subject.
- Reveals values through what a character will not say.
- Has rhythm, interruption, silence, and compression.

Always read dialogue aloud. The ear catches false rhythm faster than the eye.

If dialogue is too direct, add pressure:

- What does the character want from this exchange?
- What are they afraid would happen if they said the truth plainly?
- What object, action, or silence can carry the real emotion?

## YouTube Long-Form Scripts

YouTube scripting means planning the viewer's attention, satisfaction, and transformation. It can be word-for-word, bullet-led, slide-led, or edit-led, but it must be planned.

### When To Use Bullets vs Full Script

Use bullets when:

- The speaker is strong on camera.
- The video is conversational.
- The subject is simple.
- Authenticity matters more than precision.

Use a full script when:

- The video has a complex argument or narrative.
- Specific facts, proof, examples, jokes, or CTAs matter.
- The speaker tends to ramble.
- The edit depends on VO, clips, graphics, or timing.

### Packaging Before Script

Do not write the intro until the title promise is clear.

Define:

- Idea: one sentence.
- Viewer: who clicked and why.
- Title promise: what they expect.
- Curiosity gap: what they need to know.
- Transformation: what they will understand or feel by the end.

Thumbnail can remain loose, but title and viewer expectation must be clear.

### Intro Framework

A strong intro usually does this:

1. Confirms the click immediately.
2. Names the common belief, pain, or expectation.
3. Introduces contrast: a new angle, sharper claim, or unexpected mechanism.
4. Gives proof or credibility without rambling.
5. Gives a plan for what the video will deliver.

Every intro line should make the next line feel worth hearing.

### Body Framework

The body must beat the viewer's baseline expectations. If it only repeats common advice, the viewer leaves.

For each point, use:

- Context: what is this?
- Application: how does it work?
- Framing: why does it matter in the whole video?

Order points by retention logic:

- First body point: strong and novel.
- Second body point: even stronger if possible, to create rising value.
- Later points: maintain escalation through depth, examples, stakes, or reversal.

Between sections, rehook. End one point by making the next one necessary.

### Outro And CTA

Do not let the ending collapse into filler.

The outro should:

- Remind the viewer what changed.
- Leave them with a high note.
- Connect naturally to the next action.

Calls to action should be native. Tie the resource, next video, newsletter, product, or offer to a problem the video already created or solved.

## Video Essay And Documentary Scripts

Use a spine of question, investigation, revelation, consequence.

Good documentary/video essay scripting:

- Opens with the central tension, not background.
- Uses context only to deepen the problem.
- Alternates evidence, interpretation, and emotional stakes.
- Shows whenever clips, images, charts, or artifacts can carry the point.
- Treats examples as proof, not decoration.
- Names why this story matters now.

For reaction/breakdown formats, use a clip-commentary rhythm:

- Set up what to watch for.
- Show the clip or artifact.
- Explain the move.
- Name the viewer takeaway.
- Rehook into the next example.

---

## Short-Form Social Scripts

Short-form scripts do not have room for warm-up.

Use:

- First line: direct tension, result, question, or contradiction.
- Middle: one idea, one turn, one proof/example.
- End: payoff, reversal, action, or repeatable takeaway.

Cut anything that exists only to be polite, explanatory, or atmospheric. The setup must be the story.

## Revision Diagnostics

When revising any script, ask:

- Does the first line match or beat the premise/title expectation?
- Where does attention decay?
- Where does a beat repeat instead of escalate?
- Is the protagonist or presenter actively driving the piece?
- Is there visible/audible change every few beats?
- Are we explaining what could be shown?
- Are the best points buried too late?
- Does the ending create satisfaction rather than simply stop?
- Are format rules serving production, not showing off?
- Does the script still sound like the writer's voice?

Fix the largest structural attention problem before polishing lines.
