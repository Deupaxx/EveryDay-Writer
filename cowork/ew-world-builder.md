# EVERYDAY WRITER — WORLD BUILDER SKILL

**Read the anti-AI rules and the voice profile in `ew-master.md` before generating any prose from this skill.** This file is self-contained for the questioning and bible-generation process, but any prose it produces is still governed by the master rules, including the fabrication limit.

---

## WHAT THIS SKILL IS FOR

World-building for fiction: novels, short stories, screenplays, games, and serialized narrative. It extracts a complete, internally consistent world from the writer's ideas and produces a usable world bible, a reference document that makes every writing session faster and every creative decision easier.

The world exists to serve the story, not the reverse. Every decision made here answers one question: **does this make the story more possible, or does it just make the world more elaborate?**

Elaborate is not the goal. Usable is the goal.

This skill does not write prose. It builds the scaffold the prose hangs on. For the prose itself, use `ew-scene-structure.md` or `ew-script-writing.md`.

---

## WHEN TO USE IT

Before the first scene whenever possible. Also after drafting has already begun. Many writers discover the world by writing into it first; when that happens, reverse-map what the draft already implies and turn those discoveries into a usable bible or graph.

The expensive mistake is leaving the world only inside scattered pages and memory, which means making contradictory decisions in different scenes and then spending revision fixing inconsistencies instead of improving the prose.

You do not need to answer every question before starting. You need to answer the ones whose answers affect the first chapter. Everything else can develop as you go, provided it gets recorded when decided rather than trusted to memory. If the writer already has pages, use those pages as the first source of truth and mark missing decisions as open questions.

---

## HOW THE WORLD SERVES THE STORY

The world is not the point. The characters are the point. World-building earns its place when it does one of these:

1. **Creates conflict.** The world's rules, politics, geography, or history produce a problem the character must navigate.
2. **Reveals character.** The world's pressures expose who the character is under stress.
3. **Raises stakes.** The world makes winning more meaningful and losing more costly.
4. **Creates specificity.** A detailed world makes vague emotional events feel real.

World-building that exists only to be admired is a procrastination strategy. A writer who can explain the magic system in exhaustive detail but cannot say what the protagonist wants in chapter one is using the world to avoid the harder problem.

The hard problem is character. The world exists to make the character's problem worse.

---

## THE INTERNAL CONSISTENCY REQUIREMENT

A world only has to follow its own rules, but it has to follow them without exception. The moment it breaks its own rules for plot convenience, reader trust breaks with it, and that trust is close to impossible to rebuild.

**The rule-to-consequence test:** every rule must have a consequence if broken or avoided. A world where magic exists and nothing stops anyone using it constantly is not a world, it's decoration. What does it cost? Who controls it? What happens to people who use too much? The constraint is more interesting than the capability.

## PHASE 1: THE QUESTIONER

Conduct a structured dialogue with the writer. Questions move from large to specific.

**Rules while questioning:**

- Ask one question at a time. Never cluster them.
- If an answer raises a contradiction, flag it immediately before moving on.
- If the writer says "I don't know yet," mark it as an open question and continue. Do not push for a decision they have not made.
- Adapt the sequence to genre. Hard sci-fi needs different questions than a contemporary thriller. Skip what does not apply, add what the genre requires.
- If an answer reveals a story-breaking problem, such as a world rule that eliminates all conflict or a protagonist with no opposition, name the problem and ask how they want to handle it.

### Level 1 — World concept

1. What is the genre and tone, and where does it sit on the dark-to-light spectrum?
2. What is the central premise in one sentence? Not the plot, the world condition that makes the plot possible. "A city where memories can be bought and sold."
3. What is the time period? Historical, contemporary, future, alternate history, atemporal?
4. What is the primary setting, the place where most of the story happens?
5. What is the emotional core, the feeling the reader should leave with?

### Level 2 — The protagonist

6. Who is the protagonist? Name, approximate age, one-sentence description of their situation at the start.
7. What do they want? A specific, concrete goal they are actively pursuing, not a vague desire like "peace."
8. What do they need? The internal truth they must confront. Usually different from the want, often in conflict with it.
9. What is their Ghost, the past event or wound that shapes how they move through the world? They may not consciously know it. The reader will feel it.
10. What is their fatal flaw, the thing about them that will cause their biggest problems?

### Level 3 — Conflict and opposition

11. Who or what is the primary antagonist? A person, force, system, internal demon, or a combination.
12. How does the antagonist specifically attack the protagonist's weakness? The best antagonists are targeted, not merely powerful. They expose exactly what the protagonist cannot face.
13. What are the stakes? What is lost on failure, and is it personal enough to matter?
14. What does the ordinary world look like before the inciting incident? What is normal for them?
15. What is the inciting incident, the specific event that makes the status quo impossible?

### Level 4 — World rules

*Adapt to genre. Skip or replace what does not apply.*

**If the world has magic or supernatural elements:**
16. What is the source of the power?
17. What are the limits and costs? A system with no limits has no tension.
18. Who has access, who is denied, and why?
19. What happens to those who use too much, or use it wrongly?

**If the world has advanced or alternate technology:**
20. What is the central technology that defines this world differently from ours?
21. Who controls it, and what does that control mean for everyone else?
22. What has it made possible that wasn't before, and what has it made obsolete?

**If the world is contemporary or historical:**
23. What is different about this version of the world from the world as it is or was?
24. What real-world tensions does this story engage with?

### Level 5 — Society and structure

25. What is the dominant social and political structure? Who holds power, and how do they hold it?
26. What is the primary division in this society: class, faction, belief, geography, biology? How does it affect the protagonist directly?
27. What do ordinary people believe about how the world works, and what is the truth that contradicts it?
28. What does the protagonist's world smell like, sound like, and feel like on a normal day? Sensory grounding for the writer, not the reader.

### Level 6 — History and mythology

29. What happened in this world's past that still shapes the present? The wound in the world's history, equivalent to a character's Ghost.
30. What do people believe about their origins or purpose?
31. What event, person, or object is considered sacred or untouchable, and why?
32. What secret does this history contain that would change everything if known?

### Level 7 — Supporting characters

33. Who are the two or three most important supporting characters? For each: name, relationship to the protagonist, and what they want from the protagonist.
34. Who is most likely to betray the protagonist, and what would drive them to it?
35. Who does the protagonist trust most, and is that trust warranted?

### Level 8 — Open questions

Compile every decision the writer has not made. These go into the bible as named uncertainties, not blanks.

> **[Open: Magic cost mechanism]** — No decision yet on the physical cost of using the Binding. Must be resolved before writing any scene where a character uses it. Options discussed: physical aging, memory loss, emotional numbness.

Open questions are not failures. They are creative work deferred to the right moment. The failure is not knowing they are open.

### Before generating the bible

Summarize the answers back to the writer and confirm:

- The premise is internally consistent
- The protagonist has a clear want and need that are in tension
- The antagonist specifically targets the protagonist's weakness
- The world's rules have consequences
- All open questions are named

## PHASE 2: THE WORLD BIBLE

Generate a structured reference document from the answers. Use this skeleton, omitting sections the genre does not need.

```markdown
# [Story Title] — World Bible
Last updated: [date]

## 1. PREMISE
One-sentence world condition. Genre, tone, time period, primary setting.
Emotional core: what the reader should leave with.

## 2. PROTAGONIST
Name, age, situation at open.
Wants: [concrete goal]
Needs: [internal truth]
Ghost: [defining past wound]
Fatal flaw: [what causes their biggest problems]

## 3. ANTAGONIST AND OPPOSITION
Who or what. How it specifically targets the protagonist's flaw.
Stakes on failure. Why they are personal.

## 4. THE TURN
Ordinary world before. Inciting incident. Why the status quo becomes impossible.

## 5. WORLD RULES
Each rule stated with its cost and its consequence for breaking it.
| Rule | Cost | Consequence if broken or avoided |

## 6. SOCIETY
Power structure. Primary division. What ordinary people believe.
The truth that contradicts that belief.
Sensory baseline: smell, sound, texture of a normal day.

## 7. HISTORY
The wound in the world's past. Origin beliefs. What is sacred.
The buried secret and what it would change.

## 8. SUPPORTING CAST
Per character: name, relationship, what they want from the protagonist.
Most likely betrayer and their motive. Most trusted and whether it is warranted.

## 9. OPEN QUESTIONS
[Open: topic] — what is undecided, when it must be resolved, options discussed.

## 10. CONTINUITY LOG
Decisions made during drafting that were not in the original bible.
| Date | Decision | Scene where it was established |
```

## DRAFT CARTOGRAPHY MODE: EXISTING WORK TO WORLD MAP

Use this mode when the writer has already drafted chapters, scenes, a screenplay, notes, or a full manuscript and wants the world mapped from what is already on the page.

Do not run the questioner first. The draft is the source of truth.

Extract:

- Characters: names, aliases, titles, roles, relationships, loyalties, goals, secrets, first appearances
- Places: locations, regions, buildings, routes, recurring settings, controlling powers
- Factions: houses, governments, companies, crews, cults, families, armies, guilds, movements
- Rules: magic, technology, law, taboo, social codes, geography constraints, supernatural limits
- Objects: weapons, relics, documents, devices, symbols, heirlooms, medicines, currencies
- Timeline events: backstory events, scene events, promises, betrayals, deaths, discoveries, reveals
- Cultures and religions: beliefs, rituals, ancestry, customs, taboos, conflicts
- Mysteries and open threads: unanswered questions the draft creates
- Continuity risks: contradictions, renamed entities, timeline confusion, impossible geography, unstable rules

Classify every extracted fact:

- **Confirmed:** directly stated or unambiguously shown in the draft.
- **Strong inference:** not stated, but strongly implied by repeated context.
- **Question:** possible, ambiguous, contradictory, or under-supported.

Never invent missing lore. Do not invent surnames, ages, powers, geography, faction hierarchy, backstory causes, relationships, dates, or motivations. Use `[OPEN: ...]` for gaps.

### Mapping passes

1. **Source inventory.** List the chapters, scenes, files, or sections read; note POV/focal character, major entities introduced, and major events.
2. **Entity extraction.** Canonicalize names, record aliases, first appearances, confirmed facts, inferences, and open questions.
3. **Relationship extraction.** Capture graph edges: family, affiliation, power, place, conflict, dependency, knowledge, and causality. Mark each edge `confirmed`, `inferred`, or `question`.
4. **Continuity extraction.** Record durable facts future writing must respect: dates, ages, travel times, who knows what, who has met whom, injuries, deaths, promises, rule limits, geography facts, and object transfers.
5. **Conflict and gap audit.** Flag contradictions, possible duplicate entities, rule instability, timeline issues, unexplained relationship changes, and unresolved threads.

### Report format

```markdown
# Draft Cartography Report: [Story Title]

## Source Coverage
[What was read, what was not read, and any limits.]

## Character Map
| Character | Role | First seen | Key links | Open questions |

## Place Map
| Place | Type | First seen | Connected entities | Open questions |

## Faction / Group Map
| Faction | Function | Members | Territory / influence | Open questions |

## Rule / System Map
| Rule | What is confirmed | Cost / limit | Contradictions |

## Object Map
| Object | Owner / location | Story function | Transfers |

## Timeline
| Order | Event | Source | Involved |

## Relationship Edges
- [[Entity A]] -> relationship -> [[Entity B]] (confirmed/inferred/question; source)

## Continuity Facts
- [Source] Fact.

## Contradictions and Open Threads
- [Issue] Sources, problem, possible resolution.

## Recommended Next Questions
1. [The highest-leverage question to answer before drafting further.]
```

If the user wants Obsidian or graph output, create linked Markdown notes rather than only a report: story hub, world bible, continuity log, open questions, idea box, draft map report, and entity notes for characters, places, factions, rules, cultures, religions, timeline events, and important objects. Use wiki links for graph edges and preserve user-written notes when updating an existing folder.

## USING THE BIBLE DURING DRAFTING

The bible is a living document. Two rules keep it useful:

**Read before writing.** Check the relevant sections before each session. This is faster than fixing contradictions in revision.

**Write back after deciding.** Any world detail invented mid-scene goes into the Continuity Log the same session. A detail that lives only in a draft chapter will contradict something within three chapters.

When a new decision contradicts the bible, the writer chooses which one holds. Then update the bible so it stays the single source of truth. Never leave both versions standing.
